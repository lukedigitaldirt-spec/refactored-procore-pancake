# Change-communication planning

A model (ADKAR, Kotter) tells you *what change state* you are trying to create;
a stakeholder grid tells you *who* and *how closely*. This reference closes the
loop: how to turn those into an actual communication plan — messages, senders,
channels, and timing — and the practices that make internal comms land.

## The planning spine (five questions per message)
For every communication, answer:
1. **Audience** — which stakeholder segment (from the power/interest grid)?
2. **Objective** — which ADKAR block are we building (usually Awareness or
   Desire for announcements; Knowledge/Ability for rollout)?
3. **Message** — the one thing they must take away, in their language, with the
   WIIFM made explicit.
4. **Sender** — who delivers it. Prosci's "preferred sender" research: the
   *why* comes from a senior sponsor; the *what it means for me* comes from the
   employee's direct manager.
5. **Channel & timing** — matched to the quadrant's cadence, sequenced so
   sponsors and managers are briefed *before* the all-hands, never after.

## Sequencing the announcement
A botched sequence destroys trust faster than a weak message. The reliable
order:
1. Brief the guiding coalition and people managers first (they must not hear it
   with everyone else).
2. Equip managers with talking points and an FAQ for their teams.
3. Make the broad announcement (town hall / all-hands / email).
4. Follow up in small-group and 1:1 settings where Desire is actually won.
5. Sustain with repetition — people need a message many times, through
   multiple channels, before it registers.

## Two-way, not broadcast
Announcements create Awareness; they rarely create Desire. Build feedback loops
— Q&A, listening sessions, pulse surveys, an always-open channel — and visibly
act on what you hear. Resistance surfaced early is cheaper than resistance that
goes underground.

## Handling resistance
Treat resistance as data about an unmet ADKAR block, not as an obstacle.
Name it, trace it to the block (is it an Awareness gap, a Desire gap, a fear
about Ability?), and respond to that block specifically.

## Measuring the comms, not just sending them
Track leading indicators: message reach and recall, sentiment in feedback,
ADKAR scores over time, adoption and usage metrics. "We sent the email" is an
activity, not an outcome.

## Message checklist
- One clear takeaway, stated first.
- WIIFM explicit for this audience.
- Honest about what is not yet known and when it will be.
- A clear next action and where to ask questions.
- Consistent with what every other channel is saying.

## Authoritative sources
1. Jeffrey Hiatt & Timothy Creasey, *Change Management: The People Side of
   Change*, 2nd ed., Prosci, 2012 (preferred-sender model).
2. John P. Kotter, *Leading Change*, Harvard Business School Press, 1996
   (communicating the vision; the 10x under-communication problem).
3. Phillip G. Clampitt, *Communicating for Managerial Effectiveness*, 6th ed.,
   SAGE, 2016.
4. Lyn Smith & Pamela Mounter, *Effective Internal Communication*, 2nd ed.,
   Kogan Page, 2008.
5. T. J. Larkin & Sandar Larkin, "Reaching and Changing Frontline Employees,"
   *Harvard Business Review*, May–June 1996 (communicate through line managers,
   face to face).
6. William Bridges, *Managing Transitions*, 4th ed., Da Capo Lifelong Books,
   2017 (the transition curve and what to communicate at each stage).
7. Daniel Yankelovich, *The Magic of Dialogue*, Simon & Schuster, 1999
   (two-way dialogue over broadcast).
8. International Association of Business Communicators (IABC), *IABC Code of
   Ethics and Global Standard of the Communication Profession*, IABC.
9. Project Management Institute, *PMBOK Guide*, 7th ed., PMI, 2021 (manage
   communications and stakeholder engagement processes).
