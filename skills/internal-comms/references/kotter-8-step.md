# Kotter's 8-Step Process for Leading Change

John Kotter's model, first set out in a 1995 *Harvard Business Review* article
and expanded in *Leading Change* (1996), describes the organization-level
sequence for large-scale transformation. Where ADKAR is the individual lens,
Kotter is the enterprise lens. This is the logic behind
`scripts/kotter_tracker.py`.

## The eight steps
1. **Create a sense of urgency** — make the case that change is necessary now;
   dismantle complacency.
2. **Build a guiding coalition** — assemble a group with enough power,
   credibility, and expertise to lead the effort.
3. **Form a strategic vision** — a clear, compelling picture of the future and
   the strategy to reach it.
4. **Enlist a volunteer army / communicate the vision** — communicate it
   relentlessly, through every channel, and model it in behavior.
5. **Enable action by removing barriers** — clear structures, processes, and
   skills gaps that block people from acting on the vision.
6. **Generate short-term wins** — engineer visible, unambiguous early wins to
   build momentum and silence skeptics.
7. **Sustain acceleration** — press harder after the first wins; do not declare
   victory too soon.
8. **Institute change** — anchor the new approaches in the culture so they
   outlast the project and its sponsors.

## The sequence matters
Kotter's central warning is that skipping or rushing steps "creates only the
illusion of speed and never produces a satisfying result." Common failures map
directly to out-of-sequence execution:
- Acting (step 5) or declaring wins (step 6) before the vision is communicated
  (step 4) leaves people pulling in different directions.
- Declaring victory (a premature step 8) before acceleration (step 7) lets the
  old culture reassert itself.
The tracker flags exactly this: a later step marked complete or in-progress
while an earlier step has not started.

## The two editions
The original eight steps are framed as a linear project. Kotter's later work
(*Accelerate*, 2014) reframes them as concurrent, continuously running
"accelerators" operating through a dual operating system — hierarchy plus
network. The step names used here follow that updated phrasing (urgency,
volunteer army, sustain acceleration).

## Using it with the tool
Record each step as not_started / in_progress / complete. The tracker returns a
weighted percent complete, the current step (lowest not-complete), the next
action, and out-of-sequence warnings. Treat the warnings as diagnostic
questions, not automatic errors — sometimes parallel work is deliberate, but it
should be a conscious choice.

## Authoritative sources
1. John P. Kotter, "Leading Change: Why Transformation Efforts Fail,"
   *Harvard Business Review*, March–April 1995.
2. John P. Kotter, *Leading Change*, Harvard Business School Press, 1996
   (updated ed. 2012).
3. John P. Kotter, *Accelerate: Building Strategic Agility for a Faster-Moving
   World*, Harvard Business Review Press, 2014.
4. John P. Kotter & Holger Rathgeber, *Our Iceberg Is Melting*, St. Martin's
   Press, 2005 (the eight steps as a fable).
5. John P. Kotter & Dan S. Cohen, *The Heart of Change*, Harvard Business
   School Press, 2002 (the "see–feel–change" evidence base).
6. Kurt Lewin, *Field Theory in Social Science*, Harper & Row, 1951
   (unfreeze–change–refreeze, the three-stage antecedent).
7. W. Warner Burke, *Organization Change: Theory and Practice*, 5th ed., SAGE,
   2017 (comparative review of change models).
8. Todnem By, "Organisational Change Management: A Critical Review," *Journal of
   Change Management*, 2005 (critique of linear n-step models).
